I built ritsulint, a linter for Japanese law.
It reads the statute XML that [e-Gov Law Search](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/) distributes and warns where provisions conflict with each other.

A statute points to other provisions by quoting their position and their wording.
An amendment rewrites only part of a provision, so when only the quoted side changes, the quotation no longer appears anywhere in the provision.
As of 2026-11-17, e-Gov holds 8,986 statutes: the Constitution, Acts, Cabinet Orders, Imperial Ordinances, Ministerial Ordinances, and Rules.
Reading the main provisions of all of them and running 13 kinds of checks takes about 90 seconds on one thread of a Core i5-8400.
ritsulint found read-as provisions in 30 statutes whose quotation appears nowhere in the quoted provision.
14 statutes also have references like "paragraph 2 of the preceding Article" that point to a paragraph that doesn't exist.
For these 44 statutes, I read each warning against the provisions and confirmed the conflict.

The list of warnings is public at [ritsulint.conao3.com](https://ritsulint.conao3.com/).
The repository is [conao3/rust-ritsulint](https://github.com/conao3/rust-ritsulint), under the Apache-2.0 license.

# A read-as quotation that isn't in the provision

Japanese statutes have read-as provisions of the form "the term 'A' in Article X shall be read as 'B'".
They let the same provision serve in another situation.
A is a copy of the exact wording of the quoted provision.

[Article 13, paragraph 7 of the Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/326CO0000000319#Mp-Ch_3-Se_3-At_13-Pr_7) quotes the following wording to apply [Article 41, paragraph 1](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/326CO0000000319#Mp-Ch_5-Se_2-At_41-Pr_1) to provisional landing.

> 三十日以内とする。但し、主任審査官は、やむを得ない事由があると認めるときは、三十日を限り延長することができる。

In my translation, it reads "The period shall be within thirty days; provided, however, that the supervising immigration inspector may extend it by up to thirty days on finding that there are unavoidable circumstances."
The current Article 41, paragraph 1 writes "provided, however" as ただし in hiragana, and 但し doesn't appear in it.
ritsulint reports this conflict like this:

![img](../../../../resources/public/img/2a0865-warning.png)

Checking all past versions of each statute shows which version a warning starts from.
This warning starts from the version in force from 2024-06-10.
Act No. 56 of 2023 rewrote Article 41 and left the quotation in Article 13 as it was.
In the same way, the version history shows that 30 warnings come from a change to the quoted side alone.


# "The preceding Article" points to a different article

Statutes also point to provisions by relative position, as in "paragraph 2 of the preceding Article", "the following paragraph", or "the preceding three items".
An amendment that inserts an article in between makes the same words point to a different article.

[Article 46 of the Ordinance for Enforcement of the Railway Business Act](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/362M50000800006#Mp-Ch_3-At_46) says "the drawing showing the location of the ropeway facilities under paragraph 2, item 1 of the preceding Article".
Article 45, paragraph 2 is the provision that requires the drawing.
An amendment in force from 2019-09-14 inserted Article 45-2 in between, and "the preceding Article" now points to Article 45-2, which has only one paragraph.

In a program, adding one function doesn't change which function a call reaches.
A statute points to provisions by their position, so an amendment that adds an article breaks those references.

ritsulint sorts warnings into 3 levels of confidence.
Warnings I confirmed by reading the provisions and warnings that the structure of the provisions alone decides, such as numbers and tables of contents, are "high", and there are 139 of them.
The rest include false positives.
The two checks in the last row of the table often pick up other words or common words, so all of their warnings are "low".

ritsulint doesn't check whether a reference that writes out an article number points to an article that exists.
I couldn't find a way for a machine to decide whether "Article 10, paragraph 2" means an article of the statute itself or of another statute named a little earlier.
Context also decides which statute a position belongs to, and that is where statutes differ from name resolution in programs.


# Usage

With Nix, you can check one statute XML file.

```shell
nix run github:conao3/rust-ritsulint -- law check-law \
  --xml-file 326CO0000000319_20240610_505AC0000000056.xml \
  | jq '.Warnings[] | select(.Rule == "read-as-quote") | {Id, Status, Source, Targets, Confidence}'
```

The version of the Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act in force from 2024-06-10 gives one read-as quotation warning.

```json
{
  "Id": "bd316ca07e22167f",
  "Status": "absent",
  "Source": {
    "Article": "13",
    "Paragraph": 7
  },
  "Targets": [
    {
      "Article": "41",
      "Paragraph": 1
    }
  ],
  "Confidence": "high"
}
```

ritsulint computes `Id` from the statute, the check, the article and paragraph, and the quoted wording, without the version.
Matching the output of two versions by ID shows the warnings that the amendments between them added.
The previous version, in force from 2024-04-01, doesn't give this warning.

To check all statutes at once, pass the directory where you extracted e-Gov's [bulk download](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/bulkdownload/) and a reference date.
Reading and checking the 8,986 statutes takes about 90 seconds.

```shell
nix run github:conao3/rust-ritsulint -- corpus check-corpus \
  --xml-dir path/to/xml --as-of 20261117
```


# Rules for reading provisions in Lean

I wrote the rules for reading provisions, such as kanji numerals, splitting read-as provisions, and resolving "the same Article" and "the preceding paragraph", in Lean 4.
`decide` checks their output on real provisions, and Lean's kernel verifies it.
In this post I call one "the term 'A' shall be read as 'B'" pair of a read-as provision a clause.
For Article 13, paragraph 7 of the Immigration Control Act, the checks confirm that its 4 read-as clauses point to Article 40, Article 40, Article 41 paragraph 1, and Article 41 paragraph 3 with Article 42 paragraph 1.
[leanfran](https://s.conao3.com/blog/2026/21ebbd) compiles the Lean code to Rust, and the Rust code that loads the XML and matches the quotations calls it.

ritsulint gives no warning for wording it can't resolve.
Of the 59,659 read-as clauses, it resolved 21,848 to an article and paragraph of the same statute and matched them.
Of the clauses that point to an article of another statute, it matches only the 1,233 where the statute name or statute number identifies the statute.


# Defining the meaning of read-as provisions and proving a theorem

A read-as provision lists several clauses in one paragraph, so the way you apply the clauses can change the reading.
[Paragraph 4](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/325AC1000000100#Mp-Ch_5-At_34_2-Pr_4) of [Article 34-2 of the Public Offices Election Act](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/325AC1000000100#Mp-Ch_5-At_34_2) reads [paragraph 3](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/325AC1000000100#Mp-Ch_5-At_34_2-Pr_3) for the case where the head of a local government and the members of its assembly swap places, and the 3rd to 5th of its 10 clauses are these.

> 「<strong>長の任期満了による選挙</strong>」とあるのは「<strong>議会の議員の任期満了による一般選挙</strong>」と、「議会の議員が任期満了以外の事由によりすべてなくなつた」とあるのは「長が任期満了以外の事由により欠け、又は退職を申し出た」と、「<strong>議会の議員の任期満了による一般選挙</strong>」とあるのは「<strong>長の任期満了による選挙</strong>」と

In my translation, the clauses read: "election at the expiry of the head's term" shall be read as "general election at the expiry of the assembly members' terms", "all the assembly members have ceased to hold office for a reason other than the expiry of their terms" shall be read as "the head has left office, or has given notice of resignation, for a reason other than the expiry of the term", and "general election at the expiry of the assembly members' terms" shall be read as "election at the expiry of the head's term".

The 3rd and 5th clauses only swap two terms, but "general election at the expiry of the assembly members' terms" is both a term the 3rd clause writes and a term the 5th clause quotes.
So if you apply the clauses to the provision one at a time in the order the paragraph lists them, the 5th clause turns the "general election at the expiry of the assembly members' terms" that the 3rd clause wrote back into "election at the expiry of the head's term".
The two terms appear 3 times in total in the first half of paragraph 3, they all become "election at the expiry of the head's term", and the sentence then says that the local government holds an election for the head when the head leaves office, which makes no sense.
The only way to read this paragraph is to apply all the clauses at once to the original paragraph 3 and not to read again the terms the clauses wrote.

So I defined the meaning of read-as provisions in Lean.
The definition reads the provision once from left to right, and where a clause's quoted wording appears, it writes that clause's replacement wording.
If the quotations of several clauses sit at the same place, it takes the longer one.
It doesn't read again the wording that a clause wrote.
ritsulint compiles this definition to Rust and runs it.

I also defined in Lean the condition under which replacing clause by clause gives the same text as this definition.
The condition has two parts: no clause's quotation overlaps the quotation of an earlier clause, and no clause's quotation appears in the wording that an earlier clause wrote.
The following theorem proves that, for all clauses and all provisions, the two ways of reading give the same text when the clauses meet the condition.

```lean
theorem applySeq_eq_applyAll (cs : List (Clause α)) (t : List α)
    (h : independent cs t = true) : applySeq cs t = applyAll cs t
```

The statutes on e-Gov have 4,854 places where one paragraph applies two or more clauses to the same article or paragraph.
In 4,602 of them, the clauses meet the condition, and replacing them one at a time gives the same text.
In 219 places, replacing the clauses one at a time changes the text, and Article 34-2 of the Public Offices Election Act is one of them.
There were 0 places where the clauses meet the condition and the text still changes.

In some places, you can read the text in more than one way.
[Article 30 of the Act on Protection of the Names of Specific Agricultural, Forestry and Fishery Products and Foodstuffs](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/426AC0000000084#Mp-Ch_4-At_30) reads 変更の登録 ("registration of a change") in [Article 3, paragraph 2](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/426AC0000000084#Mp-Ch_2-At_3-Pr_2) as 指定の変更 ("change of a designation"), and 登録の日 ("date of registration") as 指定の日 ("date of designation").
The provision has 変更の登録の日 ("date of registration of a change"), and the two quotations overlap at 登録 ("registration").
Reading from the left gives 指定の変更の日 ("date of change of a designation"), and reading from the right gives 変更の指定の日 ("date of designation of a change").
There are 12 places where the direction of reading changes the text, and ritsulint reports them as warnings.

[lean-leanassay](https://github.com/conao3/lean-leanassay) checks whether the theorem stops holding when I make small breaks in the functions of the definition.
It makes 54 broken versions and confirms that the theorem stops holding for all of them.
In the version without one of the condition checks, the two ways of reading disagree on the swapping clauses.

I haven't proved the code that takes provisions out of the XML, the code that splits a paragraph into clauses, or the compilation from Lean to Rust.
Matching whether a quotation is in a provision is also a string comparison in Rust, outside the proof.
The definition fixes the way of reading as left to right, taking the longer quotation.

35 of the "high" read-as warnings carry a record in which Lean's kernel computed that applying the read-as clauses to the quoted provision leaves the text unchanged.
Each record checks one case, so I call them certificates to keep them apart from the theorem.
The definitions and proofs are in [ReadAs.lean](https://github.com/conao3/rust-ritsulint/blob/master/lean/Ritsulint/ReadAs.lean), and the certificates are in [Findings.lean](https://github.com/conao3/rust-ritsulint/blob/master/lean/Ritsulint/Findings.lean).


# What a warning tells you

A warning shows a conflict in e-Gov's data.
The original text of the amending statute decides whether the statute as published in the Official Gazette has the error.
For 7 statutes, including the Immigration Control Act, the Regulation for Enforcement of the Radio Act, and the Order for Enforcement of the Long-Term Care Insurance Act, I confirmed that the original text of the amending statute doesn't fix the quotation either.
The warning in the Load Line Regulations is a difference between full-width and half-width alphanumeric characters, which I think came from turning the statute into data.

A person reading a read-as quotation that is one character off from the provision can still tell which provision it means.
Even so, a machine can check whether a quotation is in the provision, and the check finishes in 90 seconds for all statutes on e-Gov.

If drafters run the same checks while writing an amendment bill, they will find the quotations left behind before promulgation.
